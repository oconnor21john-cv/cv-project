# Literature Review: Web Scraping Ethics and Digital Food Allergen Management Systems

## Introduction

When a 15-year-old girl died in 2016 after eating a Pret a Manger baguette containing sesame seeds not listed on the label, the tragedy sparked not only legal reform but a broader recognition that allergen information systems are failing vulnerable populations. The subsequent introduction of Natasha's Law in 2021 mandated clearer allergen labelling, yet this regulatory response addresses only packaged foods, leaving a vast digital landscape of online recipes largely unregulated and potentially dangerous for the millions managing food allergies. This gap between regulatory protection and digital reality forms the central tension explored in this review.

The question is not whether technology can help people with food allergies navigate online recipes more safely. Clearly it can, and several researchers have demonstrated technical feasibility. Rather, the more pressing and under-examined question is whether such technology should be built using web scraping, and if so, under what ethical constraints. Kelly (2024) documents a troubling 20-year trajectory of rising food allergy prevalence in England, with hospital admissions for anaphylaxis climbing steadily. Yet the academic response has been curiously bifurcated: computer scientists develop increasingly sophisticated scraping and classification algorithms with minimal engagement with health ethics, while public health researchers discuss allergen management largely without considering the technical possibilities and pitfalls of automated data collection. This review argues that neither approach is sufficient. What we need, and what the literature conspicuously lacks, is a framework that treats web scraping for health applications as fundamentally different from scraping for commercial or general research purposes, demanding higher ethical standards precisely because the stakes involve human safety rather than mere data accuracy.

## The Public Health Context of Food Allergies

### Rising Prevalence and Clinical Significance

Kelly (2024) presents what should be alarming data from a 20-year English cohort study, yet the findings arrive with a curious lack of urgency in their implications for digital health interventions. Hospital admissions for anaphylaxis have risen substantially, with food allergies driving much of this increase, particularly among children and young adults. What Kelly does not adequately address, however, is the disconnect between where allergic reactions occur (increasingly at home, using recipes found online) and where regulatory attention focuses (primarily on commercial food establishments and packaged products). This oversight matters because it reveals a blind spot in public health thinking: the assumption that better labelling alone will solve the problem, when in fact the proliferation of unregulated recipe content online may be creating new risks faster than traditional regulatory mechanisms can address them.

The clinical significance of food allergies extends beyond immediate allergic reactions to encompass broader impacts on quality of life, psychological wellbeing, and social participation. Kelly (2024) notes that individuals with food allergies face constant vigilance in food selection, often experiencing anxiety about accidental exposure and social isolation due to dietary restrictions. The study emphasises that effective allergen management requires not only medical interventions but also practical tools that enable individuals to navigate daily food choices safely and confidently. This context establishes the rationale for developing digital applications that can assist in identifying safe food options, particularly in the domain of recipe selection where ingredient information may be complex or incomplete.

### Regulatory Framework for Allergen Declaration

Anaphylaxis UK (2023) dutifully lists the 14 major allergens mandated for declaration under UK and EU law, but the organisation's guidance reveals an uncomfortable truth: these regulations were designed for a pre-digital food landscape. The list itself (cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soybeans, milk, tree nuts, celery, mustard, sesame, sulphites, lupin, and molluscs) emerged from decades of clinical evidence and lobbying, yet it assumes a world where food information flows through regulated channels like product labels and restaurant menus. Online recipes exist in a regulatory grey zone, neither clearly covered by food information regulations nor entirely exempt from them. This ambiguity creates both the opportunity and the ethical dilemma for automated allergen detection systems: they could fill a genuine safety gap, but in doing so, they might also create false confidence in unregulated information.

Anaphylaxis UK (2023) provides detailed information about each allergen group, including common sources, cross-reactivity patterns, and practical management strategies. This comprehensive framework establishes the knowledge base required for developing automated allergen detection systems. The organisation notes that allergen management is complicated by several factors including hidden allergens in processed foods, cross-contamination during food preparation, and variability in allergen labelling practices across different contexts. These challenges underscore the need for digital tools that can aggregate and analyse ingredient information from multiple sources, providing users with comprehensive allergen assessments that account for the complexity of real-world food environments.

The regulatory framework described by Anaphylaxis UK (2023) also establishes important considerations for the design of allergen-filtered recipe systems. The organisation emphasises that allergen information must be accurate, up-to-date, and presented in a clear, accessible format. Furthermore, they note that individuals with food allergies require not only information about allergen presence but also guidance about safe alternatives and strategies for adapting recipes to accommodate dietary restrictions. These requirements suggest that effective allergen management tools must go beyond simple allergen detection to provide actionable information that supports safe and enjoyable food experiences.

## Web Scraping: Technical Approaches and Methodologies

### State of the Art in Web Scraping Techniques

Khder (2021) offers a technically competent survey of web scraping methodologies, distinguishing between targeted scraping and broad crawling, but the review suffers from a problem endemic to computer science literature: it treats all scraping targets as morally equivalent. Whether one is scraping product prices, news articles, or recipe ingredients receives identical technical treatment, as though the ethical implications of automated data collection were invariant across domains. This is precisely wrong. Scraping recipe data for allergen detection is not like scraping stock prices or weather data because errors in the former can hospitalise or kill people, while errors in the latter merely inconvenience them. Yet Khder's review, like much of the technical literature, proceeds as though reliability were purely a technical concern rather than an ethical imperative that might demand different approaches for different domains.

Khder (2021) categorises web scraping techniques into several approaches including HTML parsing, DOM parsing, and vertical aggregation. HTML parsing involves analysing the raw HTML code of web pages to extract desired information, while DOM parsing works with the Document Object Model representation of web pages, allowing for more sophisticated extraction based on page structure. Vertical aggregation focuses on collecting data from multiple sources within a specific domain, which aligns closely with the requirements of recipe aggregation systems. The author notes that each approach has distinct advantages and limitations, with the choice of technique depending on factors such as the structure of target websites, the complexity of desired data, and the scale of collection activities.

Sharma and Borkar (2024) extend this technical discussion by examining dynamic web scraping strategies, which are necessary for extracting data from websites that use JavaScript to generate content dynamically. The authors conduct a comparative analysis of different techniques for handling dynamic content, including browser automation, headless browsers, and API interception. They note that many modern recipe websites employ dynamic content loading to improve user experience, but this creates challenges for traditional scraping approaches that rely on static HTML analysis. The authors propose a framework for evaluating different dynamic scraping strategies based on factors including reliability, performance, and resource requirements.

The technical considerations discussed by Sharma and Borkar (2024) have important implications for allergen-filtered recipe systems. Dynamic content loading means that ingredient information may not be immediately available in the initial HTML response, requiring scrapers to execute JavaScript or wait for content to load before extraction can occur. This technical complexity increases the computational resources required for scraping and introduces additional points of potential failure. The authors emphasise that robust scraping systems must implement sophisticated error handling and retry mechanisms to ensure reliable data collection despite these technical challenges.

### Domain-Specific Applications in Recipe Extraction

Several researchers have examined the specific challenges and opportunities of applying web scraping to recipe data. Suwalka et al. (2023) present Food Genie, a recipe search algorithm using web scraping to aggregate recipes from multiple online sources. The authors describe a system that scrapes recipe websites, extracts structured information about ingredients and cooking instructions, and provides search functionality based on available ingredients. Their work demonstrates the feasibility of automated recipe aggregation while highlighting several technical challenges including variability in recipe formatting across websites, ambiguity in ingredient descriptions, and the need for natural language processing to interpret cooking instructions.

The Food Genie system described by Suwalka et al. (2023) implements several technical innovations relevant to allergen-filtered recipe search. The authors develop algorithms for normalising ingredient names across different websites, recognising that the same ingredient may be described in multiple ways. They also implement techniques for extracting structured data from unstructured text, using pattern matching and machine learning approaches to identify ingredient quantities, preparation methods, and cooking times. These technical capabilities are essential for allergen detection, as accurate identification of allergens requires precise understanding of ingredient composition rather than simple keyword matching.

Chaudhari et al. (2020) provide foundational insights into the technical architecture of recipe-based systems through their work on ingredient and recipe algorithms using web mining and web scraping for Smart Chef systems. Their research demonstrates the evolution of this research area, exploring how web scraping can be combined with data mining techniques to create intelligent recipe recommendation systems. The authors examine challenges in extracting recipe data from diverse web sources, noting that different websites employ varying structures and formats for presenting recipe information. They propose a multi-stage approach involving web scraping for data collection, data cleaning to standardise extracted information, and machine learning for recipe analysis and recommendation. This work establishes important precedents for the technical architecture of allergen-filtered recipe systems, particularly in addressing the challenge of heterogeneous data formats across recipe websites.

## Allergen Detection and Classification in Recipe Systems

### Automated Allergen Identification Approaches

Roither, Kurz, and Sonnleitner (2022) deserve credit for attempting serious allergen classification in recipes, but their Applied Sciences paper inadvertently exposes a troubling gap between technical achievement and clinical responsibility. They report accuracy rates exceeding 90 percent for most allergen categories, presenting this as a success. From a machine learning perspective, 90 percent accuracy is indeed respectable. From a health perspective, it is potentially catastrophic. Consider what a 10 percent error rate means in practice: if someone with a severe peanut allergy uses a system with 90 percent accuracy to filter 100 recipes, statistically they will encounter 10 recipes incorrectly classified as safe. One of those errors could prove fatal. The authors acknowledge this limitation in a single paragraph before moving on, but this deserves to be the central concern, not a footnote. No amount of technical sophistication can eliminate the fundamental problem that automated systems will make mistakes, and in health applications, mistakes have consequences that accuracy metrics alone cannot capture.

The system described by Roither et al. (2022) employs a multi-layered approach to allergen detection. The first layer involves lexical analysis, identifying allergen-related terms in ingredient lists through pattern matching against comprehensive allergen dictionaries. The second layer applies semantic analysis, using word embeddings and contextual understanding to identify allergens even when they are described using non-standard terminology or appear as components of compound ingredients. The third layer implements rule-based reasoning, applying domain knowledge about ingredient relationships and food preparation to infer allergen presence even when not explicitly stated. This sophisticated approach addresses the complexity of real-world recipe data, where allergens may be present but not immediately obvious from ingredient descriptions.

Roither et al. (2022) also examine the challenge of allergen classification accuracy, conducting extensive validation against manually annotated recipe datasets. Their results indicate that automated allergen detection can achieve accuracy rates exceeding 90 percent for most allergen categories, though performance varies depending on the specific allergen and the quality of ingredient descriptions. The authors note that certain allergens, particularly those that may be present as minor ingredients or processing aids, are more difficult to detect automatically. They emphasise the importance of conservative classification approaches that err on the side of flagging potential allergens rather than missing them, given the serious health consequences of false negatives in allergen detection.

### Recent Advances in Allergen Detection Systems

More recent work by Sharma et al. (2025) examines food allergen detection and recommendation systems, presenting advances in both detection algorithms and user interface design. Published in SSRN Electronic Journal, their work explores how allergen detection systems can be integrated with recipe recommendation engines to provide personalised suggestions based on individual dietary restrictions. The authors develop algorithms that not only identify allergens in recipes but also suggest modifications or alternatives that would make recipes safe for individuals with specific allergies. This approach recognises that effective allergen management involves not just avoiding problematic foods but also finding satisfying alternatives.

Sharma et al. (2025) introduce several technical innovations in their allergen detection system. They implement a hierarchical allergen taxonomy that captures relationships between different allergens and ingredient categories, enabling more sophisticated reasoning about allergen presence. For example, their system understands that items labelled as dairy products likely contain milk allergens, even if milk is not explicitly listed as an ingredient. They also develop confidence scoring mechanisms that indicate the reliability of allergen classifications, allowing users to make informed decisions about whether to trust automated assessments or seek additional verification.

The recommendation component of the system described by Sharma et al. (2025) represents an important advance beyond simple allergen filtering. Rather than merely excluding recipes that contain problematic allergens, their system actively suggests recipes that meet dietary requirements while also considering factors such as nutritional balance, ingredient availability, and user preferences. This holistic approach to allergen management recognises that individuals with food allergies need not only safety information but also positive guidance about what they can eat. The authors validate their recommendation algorithms through user studies, demonstrating that personalised, allergen-aware recommendations significantly improve user satisfaction and dietary diversity.

### Ontological Approaches to Allergen Knowledge Representation

Trzmielewski (2024) contributes a theoretical perspective on allergen management through the development of an allergy ontology, published in Knowledge Organization. The author argues that effective allergen management systems require formal knowledge representation frameworks that capture the complex relationships between allergens, ingredients, foods, and allergic reactions. An ontology-based approach enables more sophisticated reasoning about allergen presence and risk, moving beyond simple keyword matching to incorporate domain knowledge about food composition, cross-reactivity, and individual sensitivity patterns.

The utility-based contextual approach proposed by Trzmielewski (2024) recognises that allergen management is inherently contextual, depending on factors such as the severity of individual allergies, the likelihood of cross-contamination in different food preparation contexts, and the availability of allergen information. The author develops a formal ontology that represents these contextual factors, enabling allergen management systems to provide personalised risk assessments rather than one-size-fits-all classifications. This approach acknowledges that individuals with food allergies have varying risk tolerances and information needs, requiring flexible systems that can adapt to different use cases.

Trzmielewski (2024) also examines the challenge of maintaining and updating allergen knowledge bases as scientific understanding evolves and new allergens are identified. The ontological framework provides mechanisms for incorporating new knowledge without requiring fundamental restructuring of the system, supporting long-term sustainability of allergen management tools. The author emphasises that ontology-based approaches facilitate interoperability between different allergen management systems, enabling data sharing and collaborative development of allergen knowledge resources. This perspective suggests that effective allergen management requires not just individual applications but coordinated ecosystems of tools and data sources.

## Ethical and Legal Frameworks for Web Scraping

### Comprehensive Ethical Considerations

Brown et al. (2024) have produced what is arguably the most comprehensive framework for ethical web scraping currently available, yet even this thorough treatment reveals the limitations of applying general ethical principles to health-specific applications. Their framework addresses respect for data sources, privacy protection, legal compliance, and scientific integrity, all of which are important. What it conspicuously lacks, however, is any discussion of downstream harm to end users of scraped data. The framework is built around protecting websites and their users from scraping harms, but it does not adequately consider scenarios where scraped data, even if collected ethically, might harm people who rely on it. This is not a flaw in Brown et al.'s work so much as a reflection of how web scraping ethics has developed: primarily in response to concerns about data theft and server overload, not about whether scraped information is fit for safety-critical purposes.

Brown et al. (2024) argue that web scraping exists at the intersection of technological capability and social responsibility, requiring researchers to navigate complex terrain where traditional research ethics may not provide clear guidance. The authors identify several key ethical principles that should guide web scraping activities. First, they emphasise the principle of respect for persons, arguing that even publicly accessible data may carry privacy expectations and that scrapers should consider the potential impacts of data collection on individuals whose information appears online. Second, they discuss the principle of beneficence, suggesting that web scraping should be conducted in ways that maximise benefits while minimising potential harms to data sources, individuals, and broader communities.

The framework proposed by Brown et al. (2024) also addresses institutional and scientific dimensions of ethical web scraping. They note that researchers conducting web scraping must navigate institutional policies, including requirements for ethical review and data management. The authors argue that while traditional Institutional Review Board frameworks may not directly apply to web scraping of public data, researchers should nonetheless engage with ethical review processes to ensure their activities align with institutional values and community standards. They also emphasise the scientific dimension of ethical web scraping, noting that poor quality data collection can lead to flawed research conclusions, which itself represents an ethical failure when such research informs policy or practice.

### Regulatory and Policy Frameworks

The Food Standards Agency (2022) provides important regulatory context through its web scraping policy, which establishes guidelines for scraping food-related information from government websites. The policy recognises the value of making food safety and allergen information widely accessible while also protecting the integrity of official data sources. The agency permits web scraping of publicly available information for legitimate purposes including research, public health applications, and consumer information services, provided that scraping activities do not interfere with normal website operations or violate terms of service.

The Food Standards Agency (2022) policy establishes several specific requirements for ethical web scraping of food information. Scrapers must identify themselves clearly through User-Agent strings, implement rate limiting to avoid overwhelming servers, respect robots.txt directives, and provide attribution when using scraped data. The policy also addresses data quality concerns, noting that scrapers are responsible for ensuring that extracted information accurately reflects source data and that any processing or transformation is clearly documented. These requirements reflect broader principles of transparency, accountability, and respect for data sources that characterise ethical web scraping practices.

The regulatory framework established by the Food Standards Agency (2022) has particular relevance for allergen-filtered recipe systems, as such systems may scrape food-related information from both government and commercial sources. The policy suggests that regulatory bodies recognise the public value of tools that make allergen information more accessible, while also emphasising the need for responsible data collection practices. The agency's approach of permitting scraping while establishing clear guidelines provides a model for how web scraping can be accommodated within regulatory frameworks, balancing openness with protection of data integrity.

### Commercial Perspectives on Nutritional Data Scraping

Food Data Scraper (no date) examines the role of scraping nutritional information from food websites from a commercial perspective, highlighting both the opportunities and challenges of automated nutritional data collection. The source discusses how web scraping enables the aggregation of nutritional information from diverse sources, creating comprehensive databases that can support various applications including dietary planning, nutritional analysis, and allergen management. The commercial context introduces additional considerations beyond those present in academic research, including intellectual property concerns, competitive dynamics, and sustainability of data collection practices.

The commercial perspective provided by Food Data Scraper emphasises the importance of establishing sustainable relationships with data sources. While academic research may involve one-time or limited data collection, commercial applications often require ongoing scraping to maintain current information. This sustained relationship with data sources necessitates particularly careful attention to ethical considerations, as aggressive or disruptive scraping practices could damage relationships and result in loss of access. The source suggests that commercial scrapers should consider offering value back to data sources, whether through traffic referrals, data quality feedback, or other forms of reciprocity.

Food Data Scraper also discusses technical challenges specific to nutritional data scraping, including variability in how nutritional information is presented across websites, the need for normalisation to enable comparison across sources, and the challenge of maintaining data currency as recipes and nutritional information are updated. These technical challenges have important implications for allergen-filtered recipe systems, as allergen information must be not only accurate at the time of collection but also kept current as recipes are modified or new allergen information becomes available. The commercial perspective highlights the resource requirements of maintaining high-quality scraped datasets over time.

## Synthesis and Justification for the Current Project

### Integration of Technical and Ethical Frameworks

Here we arrive at the central problem: the technical literature demonstrates feasibility while the ethical literature establishes general principles, but neither adequately addresses whether automated allergen detection systems built on scraped data should exist at all, let alone how they should be designed. This is not a gap that can be filled by simply combining existing frameworks. Suwalka et al. (2023), Roither et al. (2022), and Sharma et al. (2025) show us that we can build these systems. Brown et al. (2024) and the Food Standards Agency (2022) tell us how to scrape responsibly. But none of them seriously grapple with the possibility that a responsibly-scraped, technically-sophisticated allergen detection system might still be irresponsible to deploy if it creates false confidence in automated safety assessments.

This review argues for a more cautious position than the literature generally supports. Yes, such systems should be built, but only if they are designed from the ground up to acknowledge their limitations loudly and persistently. This means not just disclaimers buried in terms of service, but interface designs that make uncertainty visible, that require active user verification, that default to caution rather than convenience. The technical capability exists, and the public health need is real, but the gap between what we can build and what we should build remains wider than the existing literature acknowledges.

The integration of these technical and ethical frameworks addresses a genuine public health need identified by Kelly (2024) and Anaphylaxis UK (2023). Rising food allergy prevalence, combined with the complexity of managing dietary restrictions in contemporary food environments, creates demand for tools that can help individuals identify safe food options. The vast amount of recipe data available online represents an underutilised resource that, if accessed responsibly through ethical web scraping practices, can significantly improve allergen management capabilities. The project proposed in this dissertation demonstrates how technical capability can be harnessed to serve public health goals while respecting the rights and interests of data sources.

The ontological perspective provided by Trzmielewski (2024) suggests that effective allergen management requires not just data collection but also sophisticated knowledge representation and reasoning capabilities. The proposed system incorporates these insights by implementing allergen detection algorithms that go beyond simple keyword matching to incorporate domain knowledge about ingredient relationships and allergen characteristics. This approach recognises that allergen management is a complex task requiring integration of multiple knowledge sources and reasoning strategies, rather than a simple filtering operation.

### Addressing Limitations and Ensuring Safety

A critical aspect of justifying this project involves acknowledging its limitations and implementing safeguards to protect user safety. The work of Roither et al. (2022) demonstrates that while automated allergen detection can achieve high accuracy, it is not perfect. The proposed system addresses this limitation through several mechanisms. First, it implements conservative allergen flagging that prioritises false positives over false negatives, recognising that it is better to unnecessarily exclude a safe recipe than to incorrectly classify a recipe containing allergens as safe. Second, it provides clear disclaimers informing users that automated allergen detection cannot replace careful ingredient verification and consultation with healthcare providers.

The technical challenges identified by Sharma and Borkar (2024) regarding dynamic web scraping also inform the system design. The proposed system implements robust error handling and fallback mechanisms to ensure continued functionality even when scraping of particular sources fails. Mock data provides a safety net ensuring that users can access basic functionality even when live scraping is unavailable, while modular design facilitates updates to scraping logic as website structures change. These technical choices reflect a commitment to reliability and sustainability, recognising that allergen management tools must function consistently to be trustworthy.

The ethical framework proposed by Brown et al. (2024) emphasises the importance of transparency and accountability in web scraping projects. The proposed system implements these principles through clear documentation of data sources, transparent communication about allergen detection methodologies, and mechanisms for user feedback about accuracy and usability. This transparency serves multiple purposes: it enables users to make informed decisions about how much to rely on the system, it facilitates ongoing improvement through identification of errors or limitations, and it demonstrates good faith commitment to ethical data collection practices.

### Contribution to Knowledge and Practice

This project contributes to both theoretical understanding and practical application in several interconnected ways. Theoretically, it demonstrates how ethical frameworks for web scraping can be operationalised in health-related applications, providing a concrete example that may inform future projects at the intersection of data collection ethics and digital health. The project bridges the gap between abstract ethical principles and practical implementation challenges, offering insights about how ethical considerations can be integrated throughout the development lifecycle rather than treated as afterthoughts.

Practically, the project creates a functional tool that addresses the public health need identified by Kelly (2024) while implementing the technical approaches demonstrated by Suwalka et al. (2023) and Roither et al. (2022). By combining recipe aggregation through ethical web scraping with sophisticated allergen detection algorithms, the system provides capabilities that are not readily available through existing tools. The focus on the 14 major allergens identified by Anaphylaxis UK (2023) ensures that the system addresses the most clinically significant allergens, maximising public health impact.

The project also contributes to the growing body of work on digital allergen management, an area that Sharma et al. (2025) and Trzmielewski (2024) identify as requiring further development. By documenting the development process, including technical decisions, ethical considerations, and challenges encountered, the project provides a resource for other researchers and developers working in this domain. This documentation serves an educational function, making visible the complex decision-making processes involved in developing responsible health technology applications.

## Conclusion

The literature examined here tells a story of technical progress outpacing ethical reflection. We know how to scrape recipe websites (Khder 2021; Sharma and Borkar 2024), we know how to detect allergens in ingredient lists with reasonable accuracy (Roither et al. 2022), and we know the public health need is real and growing (Kelly 2024). What we do not yet know, and what the literature has not adequately addressed, is whether the inevitable errors in such systems are acceptable risks given the potential benefits. This is not a question that can be answered through better algorithms or more careful scraping practices alone.

The proposed project proceeds from a position of what might be called cautious optimism: optimistic that technology can help people with food allergies navigate online recipes more safely, but cautious about the limitations and potential harms of automated systems. This stance differs from both the uncritical enthusiasm of much technical literature and the implicit skepticism of public health researchers who have largely ignored digital recipe platforms as sites of intervention. The project accepts that perfect allergen detection is impossible, that web scraping will sometimes fail, and that users will sometimes misunderstand or over-rely on automated assessments. The question is whether a system designed with these limitations in mind, that makes uncertainty visible and demands active user engagement, can still provide value while minimising harm.

This review has identified three critical gaps in the existing literature that the proposed project must address. First, the technical literature treats accuracy as a purely statistical concern rather than a threshold question about whether deployment is ethical. Second, the web scraping ethics literature focuses almost entirely on protecting data sources rather than protecting end users of scraped data. Third, the public health literature on allergen management has not engaged seriously with the possibilities and perils of automated systems built on scraped data. Filling these gaps requires not just technical competence and ethical scraping practices, but a fundamental rethinking of what responsible health technology looks like when perfect accuracy is unattainable and the consequences of errors are potentially severe. The proposed project attempts this rethinking, though whether it succeeds remains to be demonstrated through implementation and evaluation.
